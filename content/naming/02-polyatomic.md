# Polyatomic Ions {#polyatomic}

A **polyatomic ion** is a group of atoms, covalently bonded to each other, that carries an overall charge. It stays together as one unit in compounds and in solution. Treat it exactly like a single ion: one name, one charge.

[[polychart]]

## Patterns that make the chart easier

**-ate and -ite.** When two ions differ only by one oxygen, the one with **more O ends in -ate** and the one with **one fewer O ends in -ite**. The charge doesn't change.

- nitr**ate** NO₃⁻ and nitr**ite** NO₂⁻
- sulf**ate** SO₄²⁻ and sulf**ite** SO₃²⁻

**Per- and hypo-.** Chlorine, bromine, and iodine have four oxyanions, so two more prefixes are needed: **per-** for one more O than -ate, and **hypo-** for one fewer O than -ite.

[[oxyseries]]

**Hydrogen and bi-.** Adding H⁺ to an ion with a 2− or 3− charge makes a new ion with one less negative charge. Its name adds **hydrogen**: CO₃²⁻ carbonate becomes HCO₃⁻ **hydrogen carbonate**. The older prefix **bi-** means the same thing, so hydrogen carbonate is also **bicarbonate**. Phosphate can add two H⁺: PO₄³⁻ phosphate, HPO₄²⁻ hydrogen phosphate, H₂PO₄⁻ **dihydrogen** phosphate. As the number of H changes, so do the name and the charge.

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Phosphate buffers your cells.** Hydrogen phosphate and dihydrogen phosphate trade an H⁺ back and forth to hold the pH inside your cells steady, the same way bicarbonate does in your blood. You'll see how buffers work later in the course.
</div>

<div class="note" markdown="1">
**Inside a polyatomic ion.** The atoms in a polyatomic ion share electrons, and most of them spread their charge over several oxygens by resonance. See [Resonance](index.html#resonance) for carbonate, bicarbonate, and nitrate, and [Beyond the Octet](shape.html#octet) for sulfate and phosphate.
</div>
