# Start Here {#naming}

Every compound has a name and a formula, and you'll need to go from either one to the other. A medication label says *potassium chloride*; the IV bag says *KCl*. An antacid lists *calcium carbonate*; your bones are built partly from *calcium phosphate*. This page shows how those names are put together, so you can read a name and write the formula, or look at a formula and name it.

<div class="note" markdown="1">
**You don't need to memorize the charts.** Ion charges and polyatomic ions are listed here so you can look them up. Use them while you practice. The patterns will stick as you go, and the charts will still be here when they don't.
</div>

## First question: ionic or covalent?

Every naming problem starts with the same decision, because the two kinds of compounds are named differently.

| Look at the formula or name | It's… | Named with |
|---|---|---|
| A **metal** with a nonmetal, or with a polyatomic ion | **ionic** | ion names, no prefixes |
| **Ammonium** (NH₄⁺) with any anion | **ionic** | ion names, no prefixes |
| **Two nonmetals** | **covalent** | prefixes: mono-, di-, tri-… |
| **H** first, in water | **an acid** | -ic acid or -ous acid |

Metals are on the left side and in the middle of the periodic table; nonmetals are in the upper right, plus hydrogen. If a metal is in the formula, it's ionic.

## How this page fits with the rest of the site

Ionic compounds are held together by the attraction between positive and negative ions. Covalent compounds share electrons, and their Lewis structures are on the [Lewis Structures](index.html) page. Polyatomic ions are both at once: covalent inside, with an overall charge. Their Lewis structures are in [Resonance](index.html#resonance).
