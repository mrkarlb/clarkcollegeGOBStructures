# Naming Covalent Compounds {#covalent}

When two nonmetals bond, they share electrons, and the same two elements can combine in several ratios: CO and CO₂, NO and NO₂. There are no charges to balance, so the name has to say **how many of each atom** there are. Covalent names use **prefixes**. You'll find them here and in your Chemfolio.

[[prefixes]]

## The rules

1. **Name the first element** in the formula, with a prefix for how many there are. **If there's only one, leave out mono-:** CO₂ is carbon dioxide, not monocarbon dioxide.
2. **Name the second element with -ide**, and **always** give it a prefix, even mono-: CO is carbon **mon**oxide.
3. **Drop the a or o at the end of a prefix before "oxide":** mon**oxide**, tetr**oxide**, pent**oxide**. (Current IUPAC rules actually prefer *tetraoxide*, keeping only *monoxide*, but most textbooks still drop the vowel, and so does this page.)

[[covalent co co2 n2o no n2o4 p2o5 pcl5 sf6]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it: N₂O</summary>

1. **Ionic or covalent?** N and O are both nonmetals, so it's covalent: use prefixes.
2. **First element:** 2 N, so **dinitrogen**.
3. **Second element:** 1 O, so mono- plus oxide. Drop the o of mono before oxide: **monoxide**.
4. **Name:** **dinitrogen monoxide**. In medicine and dentistry it's called **nitrous oxide**, or laughing gas.
</details>

## Common names

Some covalent compounds are almost always called by their common names. Use these instead of prefix names.

[[commonnames]]

<div class="warn" markdown="1">
**Prefixes are only for covalent compounds.** If a metal is in the formula, it's ionic and the name has no prefixes. MgCl₂ is magnesium chloride. N₂O₄ is dinitrogen tetroxide. Decide which kind of compound you have first.
</div>

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Small molecules, big effects.** **Carbon monoxide** binds hemoglobin and keeps it from carrying oxygen. **Nitrogen monoxide**, usually called nitric oxide, is a signal your body makes to relax blood vessels; nitroglycerin works by releasing it. Carbon monoxide's Lewis structure, and why its carbon end grabs iron, is in [Bonding Patterns](index.html#patterns).
</div>
