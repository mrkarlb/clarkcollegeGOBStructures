# Resonance {#resonance}

Sometimes a molecule can be drawn correctly in more than one way. The atoms stay in the same places, but a double bond (and the lone pairs and charges that go with it) could sit in more than one position. Each correct drawing is a **resonance form**, and the drawings are connected by a double-headed arrow, ↔.

## Ozone

You'll build ozone in lab. It's the classic example.

[[resonance o3a o3b]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it</summary>

1. **Count:** {{ve o3a}} valence electrons.
2. **Steps 1 and 2:** put one O in the center, single-bonded to the other two: O–O–O. The central O used both of its single electrons on those bonds, so it has two pairs left. Each outer O has one single electron and two pairs.
3. **Step 3 gets stuck:** the two single electrons are on the two outer O, which aren't bonded to each other, so they can't form a bond.
4. **Fix it:** move one outer O's single electron over to the other outer O, where it pairs up as a third lone pair. That O now has one bond and three lone pairs (8 electrons). The first outer O is left with one bond and two lone pairs, only 6 electrons, so the central O shares one of its lone pairs with it as a second bond: **O=O**.
5. **Formal charges from the pattern table:** the central O has three bonds and one lone pair: **+1**. The singly bonded O has one bond and three lone pairs: **−1**. The doubly bonded O has two bonds and two lone pairs: no charge. Every O has 8 electrons.
6. **Which outer O gets the double bond?** Either one. Both drawings are correct, so ozone has **two resonance forms**.
</details>

<div class="note" markdown="1">
**What resonance means.** Ozone does not flip back and forth between the two forms. The real molecule is a single structure that is a **blend** of both, sometimes called a resonance hybrid. Its two O–O bonds are identical, and each is in between a single and a double bond in length and strength. The ↔ arrow means "these are two drawings of one molecule"; it is not the ⇌ arrow used for a reaction going back and forth.
</div>

In the upper atmosphere, ozone absorbs ultraviolet light that would otherwise damage skin and eyes. At ground level, it is a pollutant that irritates the lungs and worsens asthma. Ozone is also a surprise when it comes to [polarity](shape.html#polarity): it's made of one element, yet it is slightly polar.

### Drawing resonance forms

- **Move only electrons**, meaning lone pairs and the second or third lines of multiple bonds. **Never move atoms.**
- **Every form has the same total number of electrons**, and every form must be a correct Lewis structure on its own, with an octet on every C, N, O, and F.
- **Draw all the forms**, joined by ↔.

## Polyatomic ions rely on resonance

Most polyatomic ions are **oxoanions**: a central atom surrounded by oxygens, carrying a negative charge. Nitrate, carbonate, sulfate, and phosphate are all oxoanions. In nearly all of them, resonance spreads the negative charge over several oxygen atoms instead of leaving it on one. Oxygen is the most electronegative atom in these ions, so it holds negative charge well, and **spreading the charge over several oxygens is what makes these ions stable**.

### Carbonate and bicarbonate

[[resonance co3a co3b co3c]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it</summary>

1. **Count:** {{ve co3a}} valence electrons (4 + 3 × 6, plus 2 for the 2− charge).
2. **The structure:** C in the center with one C=O double bond and two C–O single bonds. C has four bonds and no charge.
3. **Formal charges:** each singly bonded O has one bond and three lone pairs: **−1**. Two of them make the 2− charge.
4. **Resonance:** the double bond can go to any of the three O, so carbonate has three resonance forms.
</details>

Nitrate, NO₃⁻, also has three resonance forms. Try it in the practice problem at the end of this section.

[[resonance hco3a hco3b]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it</summary>

1. **Count:** {{ve hco3a}} valence electrons (1 + 4 + 3 × 6, plus 1 for the − charge).
2. **The structure:** like carbonate, but one O carries an H. That O already has its usual two bonds (to C and to H) and two lone pairs.
3. **Resonance:** the double bond and the −1 charge are shared between the **two O without an H**, giving **two resonance forms**. Why not put the double bond on the O that holds the H? That O would have three bonds and a +1 charge, and both other O would be −1. That drawing creates extra charges instead of spreading one out, so it isn't counted as a resonance form.
</details>

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Bicarbonate in the blood.** Most of the carbon dioxide your tissues produce travels to the lungs as bicarbonate, dissolved in blood plasma. Bicarbonate is also the blood's main buffer, holding blood pH close to 7.4. You'll see how buffers work later in the course.
</div>

### Every carboxylate

[[resonance ac_a ac_b]]

Acetate has the same two-form resonance as bicarbonate: the double bond and the −1 charge are shared between two oxygens. **Every carboxylate group, –COO⁻, works this way**, including pyruvate and lactate in metabolism and every amino acid at body pH. CHEM&131 students will see this pattern constantly.

## Where this goes next

A finished Lewis structure is the starting point for everything on the next page. Count the electron groups around the central atom and you have its **shape**. Add electronegativity and you have its **polarity**. Polarity decides how molecules attract each other, which explains boiling points, what dissolves in water, and how medicines move through the body.
