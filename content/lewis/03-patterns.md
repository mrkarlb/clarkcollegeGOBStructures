# Bonding Patterns and Formal Charge {#patterns}

Sometimes Step 3 gets stuck. You pair up every single electron you can, and an atom is still short of an octet. When that happens, a **neighboring atom shares one of its lone pairs** as an extra bond. The atom that gives up a lone pair ends up with more bonds than usual, and an atom can also end up with fewer. Either way, it carries a **formal charge**.

This comes up early and often. Ions such as ammonium and hydronium, greenhouse gases such as carbon monoxide, ozone, and nitrous oxide, and almost every polyatomic ion work this way.

## The pattern table

You don't need a formula to find formal charges. For C, N, and O, a few **bonding patterns** cover nearly everything you'll see. Count an atom's bonds (a double bond counts as two) and its lone pairs, then match it to the table.

[[patterns]]

- **Usual number of bonds: no charge.** C makes 4 bonds; N makes 3 bonds and keeps 1 lone pair; O makes 2 bonds and keeps 2 lone pairs.
- **One more bond than usual: +1.** The atom shared one of its lone pairs, so it gave up a share of those electrons.
- **One fewer bond than usual: −1.** The atom kept an extra lone pair.

Every pattern in the table has 8 electrons. Formal charge doesn't change the octet; it records how the octet was reached.

<div class="warn" markdown="1">
**Nitrogen with four bonds is still an octet.** Four bonds are 8 electrons, exactly an octet. An N with four bonds isn't an exception and hasn't "expanded" anything. It's N⁺: nitrogen that has shared its lone pair. **C, N, O, and F never have more than 8 electrons.** Their outer shell has only s and p orbitals, which hold 8 electrons, so 8 fills it. Larger atoms such as S and P can go beyond 8; see [Beyond the Octet](shape.html#octet).
</div>

<div class="note" markdown="1">
**If your textbook uses a formula.** Formal charge = (valence electrons) − (lone-pair electrons) − (number of bonds). For N in ammonium: 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. The pattern table gives the same answer without the arithmetic.
</div>

The formal charges in a structure always **add up to the overall charge**: zero for a neutral molecule, and the ion's charge for an ion.

## Worked examples

### Ions with H: ammonium, hydronium, and hydroxide

[[lewisrow nh4 h3o oh]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it</summary>

1. **Ammonium, NH₄⁺:** {{ve nh4}} valence electrons (5 + 4, minus 1 for the + charge). N bonds to four H, using all 8 electrons, with no lone pair left. Four bonds is one more than N's usual three, so N is **+1**, which matches the ion's charge.
2. **Hydronium, H₃O⁺:** {{ve h3o}} valence electrons (6 + 3, minus 1). O bonds to three H and keeps one lone pair. Three bonds is one more than O's usual two, so O is **+1**.
3. **Hydroxide, OH⁻:** {{ve oh}} valence electrons (6 + 1, plus 1). O bonds to one H and keeps three lone pairs. One bond is one fewer than usual, so O is **−1**.
4. **Check:** each formal charge equals the ion's charge, because each ion has only one atom that isn't H.
</details>

Ammonium forms when ammonia's lone pair picks up an H⁺. The same thing happens to the nitrogen in amino acids and many medicines, which is why so many of them carry a + charge in the body.

### Carbon monoxide: a lone pair has to be shared

[[steps co share]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it</summary>

1. **Count:** {{ve co}} valence electrons.
2. **Steps 1 and 2:** draw C–O. C has three single electrons left; O has one single electron and two pairs.
3. **Step 3:** pair C's single electron with O's: now C=O. C still has two single electrons, which pair up as a lone pair. But C has only a double bond and one lone pair, which is 6 electrons, not 8.
4. **Share a lone pair:** O shares one of its lone pairs as a third bond: **C≡O**. Now C has a triple bond and one lone pair (8 electrons), and so does O (8 electrons).
5. **Formal charges from the table:** C has three bonds and one lone pair, one fewer bond than usual: **−1**. O has three bonds and one lone pair, one more bond than usual: **+1**. They add up to zero, as they must for a neutral molecule.
</details>

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Why carbon monoxide is poisonous.** The carbon end of CO, with its lone pair and −1 charge, binds the iron in hemoglobin more than 200 times more tightly than oxygen does. Hemoglobin that is holding CO can't carry oxygen, so even small amounts of CO in the air can starve tissues of oxygen.
</div>

### Nitrous oxide: nitrogen in the center

[[lewis n2o]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it</summary>

1. **Count:** {{ve n2o}} valence electrons (5 + 5 + 6).
2. **Central atom:** one N goes in the center, N–N–O.
3. **The structure:** the central N has a triple bond to the outer N and a single bond to O, with no lone pairs. The outer N has its triple bond and one lone pair. O has one bond and three lone pairs.
4. **Formal charges:** the central N has four bonds: **+1**. O has one bond: **−1**. The outer N has its usual three bonds and one lone pair: no charge. The charges add up to zero.
5. **Another arrangement works too:** N=N=O, with the charges in different places. Molecules that can be drawn more than one correct way are the subject of [Resonance](#resonance).
</details>

Nitrous oxide is "laughing gas," the sedative used in many dental offices. It is also a potent greenhouse gas, much of it released from fertilized farm soils.

<div class="note" markdown="1">
**CHEM&131 students: you'll see these patterns everywhere in biochemistry.** O with one bond and three lone pairs (−1) is the oxygen in every carboxylate group, –COO⁻, as in acetate and pyruvate. N with four bonds (+1) is the ammonium group, –NH₃⁺, in every amino acid at body pH. Spotting them by pattern is much faster than counting electrons. [Intermolecular Forces](shape.html#imf) shows why those charges matter in the body.
</div>
