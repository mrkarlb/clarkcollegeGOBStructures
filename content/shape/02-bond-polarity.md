# Bond Polarity {#bond-polarity}

In a covalent bond, two atoms share a pair of electrons, but they don't always share it equally. **Electronegativity (EN)** measures how strongly an atom pulls on shared electrons. It increases toward the upper right of the periodic table, and fluorine is the most electronegative element.

[[en]]

## Finding a bond's polarity

Subtract the two atoms' electronegativity values. The difference, **ΔEN**, tells you the bond type:

| ΔEN | Bond type | What happens to the electrons |
|---|---|---|
| less than 0.5 | nonpolar covalent | shared about equally |
| 0.5 to 1.9 | polar covalent | shared unequally, pulled toward the more electronegative atom |
| 2.0 or more | ionic | transferred, forming ions |

In a polar bond, the more electronegative atom gets a **partial negative charge, δ−**, because the shared electrons spend more time near it. The other atom gets a **partial positive charge, δ+**. A nonpolar bond gets neither symbol.

[[bondtable C-H C=O O-H N-H C-Cl Na-Cl]]

C–Cl sits right at the 0.5 cutoff, so it counts as polar, but only slightly.

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it: the bonds in formaldehyde, CH₂O</summary>

1. **C–H:** C is 2.5 and H is 2.1, so ΔEN = 0.4. That's less than 0.5: **nonpolar**. No δ symbols.
2. **C=O:** C is 2.5 and O is 3.5, so ΔEN = 1.0: **polar**. O is more electronegative, so O is **δ−** and C is **δ+**.
3. **A double bond is still one bond for polarity.** Use the same two EN values whether the bond is single, double, or triple.
</details>

<div class="note" markdown="1">
**Values differ slightly between tables.** Some tables give more decimal places (C 2.55, H 2.20, O 3.44). Use the values your course gives you; the ones above match the values in most introductory textbooks.
</div>
