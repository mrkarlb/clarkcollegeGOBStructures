# Molecular Polarity {#polarity}

A molecule is **polar** when its electrons are pulled more toward one end than the other, leaving one side partly negative and the other partly positive. Whether that happens depends on two things you already know how to find: the **polar bonds** and the **shape**.

## Three questions

1. **Are there any polar bonds?** If not, the molecule is **nonpolar**.
2. **Is the shape balanced?** If the central atom has **no lone pairs** and **every atom around it is the same**, the polar bonds pull equally in opposite directions and cancel. The molecule is **nonpolar**.
3. **Otherwise, the molecule is polar.** Lone pairs on the central atom, or different atoms around it, leave the pulls unbalanced. Any lopsided molecule is polar.

[[polar ch2o h2o nh3]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it: formaldehyde, CH₂O</summary>

1. **Polar bonds?** Yes: C=O is polar (ΔEN 1.0). The C–H bonds are not (ΔEN 0.4).
2. **Balanced?** No. The shape is trigonal planar, but the three atoms around C aren't the same: the C=O bond pulls electrons toward O, "up" in the drawing, and nothing pulls back the other way.
3. **Result:** **polar**, with O as the δ− end and the C–H side as the δ+ end.
</details>

## Polar bonds, nonpolar molecules

[[polar co2 ch4 ccl4 chcl3]]

Carbon dioxide has two polar C=O bonds, but they point in exactly opposite directions and cancel, so CO₂ is nonpolar. Tetrachloromethane (CCl₄) does the same thing in three dimensions: four identical polar bonds arranged symmetrically. Replace one Cl with an H, and the balance is gone: trichloromethane (CHCl₃) is polar.

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Nonpolar doesn't mean harmless to the climate.** CO₂ and CH₄ are nonpolar, yet both are greenhouse gases. As they bend and stretch, their bonds shift out of balance for an instant, and those moving partial charges absorb infrared light (heat) radiated from Earth's surface. N₂ and O₂, which make up 99% of the air, have no polar bonds at all, so they can't absorb heat this way. You'll measure this in the greenhouse gas lab.
</div>

## Ozone: a polar molecule made of one element

[[polar o3a]]

Every bond in ozone is O–O, so the course rules would call it nonpolar. But look at the Lewis structure on the first page: the central O carries a **+1** formal charge, and the −1 charge is shared between the two outer O atoms ([Resonance](index.html#resonance)). With the molecule bent, the charge isn't balanced, so ozone is **slightly polar**. It's the exception that proves the point: polarity is about how the electrons are distributed, not just which elements are present.

## Larger molecules

For molecules with more than one central atom, such as those with a carbon backbone, work through each central atom. A useful shortcut: **if a molecule with more than one carbon contains O or N, it is almost always polar**, because O–H, N–H, C–O, and C=O bonds rarely cancel in a large, lopsided molecule.
