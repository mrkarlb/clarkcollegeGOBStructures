# Beyond the Octet {#octet}

Most molecules in this course follow the octet rule. A few, built around larger atoms, don't. You won't be asked to draw these, but you'll meet them in lab and later courses, and they show how far the same drawing rules can stretch.

<div class="warn" markdown="1">
**Who can go beyond 8?** **C, N, O, and F never have more than 8 electrons.** They are small, second-row atoms, and their outer shell holds only 8. **Larger atoms from the third row down, such as P, S, Cl, and Xe, have room for more than four electron groups around them.** S and P commonly do this when they're bonded to O or F, the most electronegative atoms. Count the electron groups the same way, and VSEPR still predicts the shape.
</div>

## Sulfur hexafluoride: a greenhouse gas

[[lewis sf6]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it</summary>

1. **Count:** {{ve sf6}} valence electrons (6 + 6 × 7).
2. **The structure:** S is bonded to six F. Each F has its usual one bond and three lone pairs. S uses all 6 of its valence electrons in the six bonds, so it has no lone pairs and **12 electrons** around it.
3. **Shape:** 6 electron groups, 0 lone pairs: **octahedral**, with every F–S–F angle 90° or 180°.
4. **Polarity:** each S–F bond is polar (ΔEN 1.5), but six identical bonds arranged symmetrically cancel. SF₆ is **nonpolar**.
</details>

[[shape sf6]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**The most potent greenhouse gas.** SF₆ is used to insulate high-voltage electrical equipment. Kilogram for kilogram, it traps more than 20,000 times as much heat as CO₂, and it lasts in the atmosphere for well over a thousand years. Like CO₂, it's nonpolar but absorbs heat as its bonds stretch and bend.
</div>

## Sulfate and phosphate

[[lewisrow so4 po4]]

Sulfate and phosphate are usually drawn with S=O and P=O double bonds, which gives S 12 electrons and P 10. With the double bonds, S and P carry no formal charge, and the negative charges sit on the singly bonded oxygens, spread out by resonance just as in [carbonate](index.html#resonance). This is the way they're drawn in most textbooks and in biochemistry.

<div class="note" markdown="1">
**CHEM&131 students: phosphate is everywhere in biochemistry.** Phosphate groups link the sugars in the backbone of DNA and RNA, and the three phosphates in ATP store the energy your cells run on.
</div>

## Challenge: XeOF₄

Xenon is a noble gas, but it does form compounds with the most electronegative elements. Try drawing this one before you open the walkthrough.

[[lewis xeof4]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it</summary>

1. **Count:** {{ve xeof4}} valence electrons (8 + 6 + 4 × 7).
2. **The structure:** Xe is central. Each F makes one bond and keeps three lone pairs. O makes a double bond to Xe and keeps two lone pairs. Xe has used 6 of its 8 electrons in those bonds, so it keeps **one lone pair**.
3. **Electron groups:** five bonded atoms plus one lone pair = **6 groups**. The electron geometry is octahedral.
4. **Shape:** with one of the six positions taken by a lone pair, the atoms form a **square pyramid**: four F in a square, with O at the top and the lone pair pointing down, opposite it.
5. **Polarity:** the lone pair and the different atoms make the molecule lopsided, so XeOF₄ is **polar**.
</details>
