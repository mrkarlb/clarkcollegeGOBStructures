# Intermolecular Forces {#imf}

The bonds *inside* a molecule hold its atoms together. **Intermolecular forces** are the much weaker attractions *between* separate molecules and ions. They decide whether a substance is a gas, liquid, or solid at body temperature, what dissolves in water, and how a medicine travels through your blood. All of them come from the same idea: **opposite charges attract**, whether they are full charges on ions or partial charges (δ+ and δ−) on polar molecules.

Here are the four you need to know, strongest first.

## Ion–dipole forces

An **ion** (full charge) attracts the oppositely charged end of a **polar molecule** (partial charges). When salt dissolves in water, each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by water molecules with their δ− oxygen ends facing it, and each Cl⁻ ion is surrounded by water molecules with a δ+ hydrogen pointing at it.

[[hydration]]

Ion–dipole forces are the strongest of the four. They're why ionic compounds and charged molecules dissolve so well in water, and they're the force you'll meet most often in biology and medicine.

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Electrolytes and IV fluids.** Sodium, potassium, chloride, and bicarbonate ions travel through your blood surrounded by water molecules, held there by ion–dipole forces. Normal saline, the most common IV fluid, is 0.9% sodium chloride dissolved in water.
</div>

## Hydrogen bonding

A **hydrogen bond** forms when an H atom that is **bonded to N, O, or F** is attracted to a **lone pair on an N, O, or F** of a neighboring molecule. N, O, and F are so electronegative that they leave their H strongly δ+, and they have lone pairs for that H to point at.

[[hbond]]

<div class="warn" markdown="1">
**A hydrogen bond is not a covalent bond.** It's an attraction *between* molecules, roughly 5 to 10% as strong as a covalent bond. The covalent O–H bond inside each water molecule is the solid line; the hydrogen bond to the next molecule is the dotted one.
</div>

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Hydrogen bonds hold life together.** Hydrogen bonds hold the two strands of DNA together, pairing A with T and G with C, and they're weak enough that the strands can unzip to be copied. They also fold proteins into their working shapes. A fever that runs too high can disrupt these hydrogen bonds, which is one reason dangerously high body temperatures damage proteins.
</div>

## Dipole–dipole forces

Two **polar molecules** attract each other: the δ+ end of one lines up with the δ− end of the next. Hydrogen bonding is an especially strong kind of dipole–dipole force; ordinary dipole–dipole forces act between polar molecules that don't have an H on N, O, or F, such as formaldehyde (CH₂O) and chloroform (CHCl₃).

## Dispersion forces

**Every** molecule has dispersion forces, and they're the only intermolecular force between nonpolar molecules. The electrons in a molecule are always moving, so at any instant one side may have slightly more of them than the other. That brief, temporary dipole nudges the electrons in the next molecule, and the two attract. Dispersion forces are weak for small molecules but **grow with size**: the more electrons a molecule has, and the more surface it has in contact with its neighbors, the stronger they are. That's why methane (CH₄) is a gas, octane in gasoline is a liquid, and the long chains in fats and waxes are solids.

## Which forces does a substance have?

| Force | Acts between | Look for | Relative strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ion–dipole | an ion and a polar molecule | a charge (+ or −) in water or another polar liquid | strongest |
| Hydrogen bonding | molecules with H on N, O, or F | O–H, N–H, or F–H bonds | strong |
| Dipole–dipole | polar molecules | a polar molecule without O–H or N–H | moderate |
| Dispersion | all molecules | always present; the only force for nonpolar molecules | weak, but grows with size |

These rankings hold when the molecules are about the same size. A very large nonpolar molecule can have stronger total dispersion forces than a small polar one.

## What intermolecular forces explain {#imf-properties}

### Boiling point

To boil a liquid, its molecules have to pull away from each other. The stronger the forces between them, the more energy that takes, and the higher the boiling point.

[[lewisrow etoh dme partial]]

Ethanol and dimethyl ether have exactly the same formula, C₂H₆O. Both are polar. But ethanol has an O–H bond, so its molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other; dimethyl ether's H atoms are all on carbon, so it can't. The difference is about 100 °C: ethanol is a liquid at room temperature, and dimethyl ether is a gas.

### Solubility: like dissolves like

A substance dissolves when its particles can attract the solvent's molecules about as strongly as they attract each other. **Polar and ionic substances dissolve in polar solvents such as water; nonpolar substances dissolve in nonpolar solvents.** Salt and sugar dissolve in water. Oil, which is nonpolar, doesn't; it dissolves in other nonpolar substances.

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Why some vitamins are stored and others aren't.** Vitamin C has several O–H groups and dissolves in water, so the body stores little of it; any extra leaves in urine. Vitamins A, D, E, and K are mostly nonpolar hydrocarbon. They dissolve in fat instead, so the body stores them in the liver and fatty tissue, which is why large doses of them can build up to harmful levels.
</div>

## Charged molecules in the body {#charged}

Many molecules in your cells carry full charges, and that changes how they behave. At body pH, about 7.4, **acid groups give up their H⁺ and amine groups pick one up**. You'll see why when the course reaches acids and bases; for now, it's enough to recognize the result.

[[lewisrow pyr_acid pyruvate]]

Pyruvic acid has a –COOH group. In the body, that group loses its H⁺ and becomes a **carboxylate**, –COO⁻: the O with one bond and three lone pairs from the [bonding pattern table](index.html#patterns). The result is pyruvate. That's why your biology textbook says **pyruvate** and not pyruvic acid: in the cell, nearly all of it is the ion. The same naming rule you already know applies: nitrate, carbonate, and acetate are ions, and so are pyruvate, lactate, and citrate.

[[lewis ala]]

An amino acid has both kinds of group, so at body pH it carries **both charges at once**: the –NH₂ group picks up an H⁺ and becomes –NH₃⁺ (N with four bonds, +1), and the –COOH group becomes –COO⁻ (−1). The overall charge is zero, but each end is charged. A molecule like this is called a **zwitterion**. Those charged ends are why amino acids dissolve in blood through ion–dipole forces, and why they attract each other so strongly that they're solids that melt only above 200 °C.

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Why medicine labels say "hydrochloride."** Many medicines are amines, with an N that can pick up an H⁺. Drug makers often sell them already in that charged form, as a salt with chloride: diphenhydramine hydrochloride (Benadryl) is one example. The charged N⁺ is held by ion–dipole forces in water, so the salt dissolves far better than the neutral molecule, which helps the medicine dissolve and be absorbed.
</div>
